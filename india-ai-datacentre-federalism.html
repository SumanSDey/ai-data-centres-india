The Uneven Race

How six Indian states are bidding for the AI data-centre boom — and why the same policy toolkit is producing wildly different winners.
Author: Suman Sunder Dey Prepared as: Research Associate assignment Coverage: Maharashtra · Tamil Nadu · Telangana · Uttar Pradesh · Karnataka · Gujarat 2026

Executive summary

  • Maharashtra still holds roughly half of India's live data-centre capacity. The states now catching up aren't matching that lead with bigger subsidies alone — they're winning with timing and a handful of genuinely differentiated bets.
  • The states leading 2026 capital inflows are already India's richer states. The boom is riding on top of — and reinforcing — a regional income gap NITI Aayog itself has called worrying.
  • The jobs case is thin: roughly 100,000 data-centre jobs are projected nationally by 2030 against $126–180 billion in committed capital — over a million dollars of capital per eventual job.
  • Coastal access (cable landing stations) is necessary but far from sufficient — Kochi has it and no data-centre boom; Odisha has neither and is moving faster than most states that do.
  • India already designed a fix for the distributional problem in 2022 — a TRAI proposal for centre-funded incentive equalisation — and never switched it on.

01

Six states, three waves

How state policies differ

India's data-centre capacity crossed roughly 1.7 gigawatts (GW) in 2025, on cumulative investment commitments of about $126 billion — a figure CBRE expects to cross $180 billion in 2026 alone. The trigger isn't data centres in the generic sense; it's the AI buildout specifically. Every state government has read the same signal: land the anchor hyperscaler campus first, and you capture a multi-decade annuity of investment, tax revenue and prestige. So states are bidding, with a broadly similar toolkit — land, power tariffs, stamp duty, capital subsidies. The outcomes, though, are not similar at all. The best explanation isn't subsidy size. It's which of three waves a state entered in.

Wave 1 — the incumbent. Maharashtra's IT/ITES policy dates to 1998, refreshed in 2015 and again in June 2023. Mumbai and Navi Mumbai host India's densest cluster of international submarine cable landing stations — the single most durable advantage a location can have, because it can't be subsidised into existence elsewhere overnight. The 2023 refresh added a 100% stamp duty exemption, a permanent electricity-duty exemption, and a five-year power-tariff subsidy outside the already-saturated Mumbai Metropolitan Region. None of that created Mumbai's lead. It protected one that already existed.

Wave 2 — the 2021–22 challengers. Tamil Nadu, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh launched dedicated policies within a year of each other; Karnataka followed in 2022. Tamil Nadu leaned on Chennai's own cable-landing status and manufacturing base — L&T alone signed an ₹18,600 crore deal with the state. Telangana took the most distinctive path of the three: rather than compete on generic land-and-power terms, it built incentives specifically around AI infrastructure — GPU density, liquid cooling, training clusters. Hyderabad's live capacity more than doubled from 61 MW (2022) to 151 MW (2025), with a 1.9 GW pipeline second only to Mumbai's; AWS alone holds 46% of that live capacity and has committed $7 billion over 14 years. Uttar Pradesh chose volume over sophistication, targeting Noida and the Yamuna Expressway corridor — its original 250 MW target has since scaled to eight parks and 900 MW. Karnataka is the odd one out: its policy deliberately steers incentives outside Bengaluru toward Mysuru and Mangaluru, a congestion-driven choice that is a real part of why Karnataka's footprint (96 MW, ~15% of national capacity in 2022) has lagged its reputation as India's software capital.

Wave 3 — the late, power-secure entrant. Gujarat had no dedicated data-centre policy at all until 9 July 2026 — three weeks before this was written. Its pitch is structurally different: 69 GW of installed power capacity, 47 GW of it renewable, i.e. a genuine power surplus in a sector where every rival is fighting grid strain, paired with the Dholera semiconductor cluster and GIFT City's financial infrastructure. It targets ₹6 lakh crore in investment and 7.5–8 GW capacity, with investor interest already reported near double that.

Growth, jobs and the inequality it's riding on

What the uneven race is doing to the economy

The five states leading 2026 capital inflows by most industry forecasts — Telangana, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Uttar Pradesh — already sit disproportionately among India's richer states. Maharashtra alone contributes roughly 13.5% of national GDP; the top five GSDP states together account for nearly half the economy.

03

Beyond the six: the coastal challengers

Why coastline alone doesn't decide this

It's tempting to read the winners' map as a coastline map — Mumbai, Chennai, both port cities with subsea cable access. But India actually has cable-landing stations in five cities, not two: Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi, Tuticorin and Thiruvananthapuram. Kochi has had international cable access for years and still has no data-centre boom to show for it. Meanwhile Odisha, which has no landing station at all, out-moved several coastal rivals in 2026. Coastal access turns out to be necessary but nowhere near sufficient — power reliability, contiguous land and execution speed matter more than the coastline itself.

04

How other federations handle this

Optional: international comparison

The United States runs the same decentralised model India does — and is roughly a decade further along the same curve, which makes it a preview more than a comparison. Virginia's sales-tax exemption alone cost the state about $1.6 billion in fiscal 2025, sixteen times its original projection. The backlash has arrived fast: over 300 data-centre bills were filed across 30-plus state legislatures within six weeks in early 2026, several proposing outright moratoriums. Tellingly, one industry survey found only about 3% of operators rank tax incentives as the single most important site factor — power, land and latency dominate the real decision.

China runs the opposite model: centrally coordinated allocation. Its 2021 "East Data, West Computing" plan directs data-centre construction toward resource-rich but lagging inland provinces rather than letting demand concentrate purely on the wealthy coast. But recent assessments are sobering — several designated western hubs sit adjacent to large cities rather than truly remote, and firms remain reluctant to shift latency-sensitive AI workloads inland. Coordination has redirected some of the map; it hasn't overridden the underlying gravity toward existing demand centres.

05

Fix the distribution, not the race

My view

My view is that the competition is good for India in aggregate and bad for India in distribution — and the fix is to change what states compete on, not to end the competition. A single national policy, absent competitive pressure, would very likely have moved slower: Telangana's AI-specific tilt, Karnataka's anti-congestion tilt and Gujarat's power-surplus tilt are useful policy variation, not wasteful duplication. But left alone, the dynamic rewards states for what they already had, returns thin jobs for the capital and tax revenue foregone, and lets states externalise water and grid costs onto the same citizens the investment was meant to benefit.

1

Switch on the mechanism India already built

Operationalise TRAI's 2022 proposal — a functioning Readiness Index paired with genuine central co-financing — so Uttar Pradesh, Odisha or Rajasthan compete on a national subsidy pool rather than each state independently escalating its own package.

2

Make efficiency standards a condition, not an add-on

Every policy renewal from here should require minimum power- and water-usage thresholds and bring data centres into environmental clearance — before India reaches Ireland's position, where data centres draw over a fifth of national electricity and new construction is under moratorium until 2028.

3

Redirect the incentive rupee toward the real bottleneck

Shift a slice of incentive spend away from land and capital subsidies — which investors themselves rank as secondary — toward a skilling-linkage fund, co-financed with the operators who benefit from the tax concessions.

None of this requires slowing the race down. It requires India to stop repeating, in slow motion, mistakes the United States is already living through in real time — and to actually use the coordination tool it designed for itself in 2022.
